The Escape Plan (15:35 - 20:00)

Coker describes the escape as an "escape of opportunity" involving George McKnight. Both had been severely tortured during the Hanoi Hilton purges of 1967 and were relocated to an old power plant facility they dubbed the "Dirty Bird."



Communication: McKnight discovered he could pick his handcuffs during siesta time. This allowed them to communicate while isolated.

The Intel: McKnight had been paraded outside and, uniquely, was able to observe the layout of the local area and identify the Doma Bridge.

The Scheme: Coker formulated a plan to reach the river, swim downstream to the delta, and find a boat to navigate toward a location accessible by U.S. aircraft. They used scrap metal found at the bombed-out facility to sabotage their door locks, creating a way to wedge the door from the outside.

Navigation and Near-Discovery (20:00 - 23:25)

The Route: McKnight’s observation was critical because the route to the river was counter-intuitive, requiring them to move 100 yards in the wrong direction to clear a physical barrier and then double back.

The Inspection: A week into their preparations, guards performed an unannounced inspection. Coker notes they were "a hair’s breadth from destruction." They survived only because their room door opened flat against the wall, creating a blind spot behind the hinge where the guards never looked.

The Escape Execution (23:25 - 26:15)

They launched the escape at 10:00 PM the night following the inspection. They reached the river by midnight and swam for five hours, covering 15 miles downstream. They were recaptured by accident when a fisherman with a cane pole spotted them in a hidden hole they had dug into the river bank. Following recapture, the North Vietnamese beat them severely because they could not comprehend how the prisoners had traveled 15 miles downriver.

The Matrix: Prisoners utilized a 5x5 matrix of letters. By tapping the row number and then the column number, they could transmit any letter of the alphabet (30:00-31:00).

Rhythmic Security: The system was incredibly secure because it did not rely on complex timing like Morse Code, but rather on a simple counting sequence. Even if the guards learned the code, the prisoners developed cadences, abbreviations, and shorthand that made the messages unintelligible to anyone not initiated in their specific communication style (33:00-36:30).
